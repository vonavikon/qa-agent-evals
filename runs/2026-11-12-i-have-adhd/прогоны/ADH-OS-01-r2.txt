**The straight answer: I don't know, and no honest clinician would give you a yes or no from four sentences.** What you listed — unfinished projects, lateness, inbox chaos, focus problems — fits ADHD, but it fits several other things just as well: chronic sleep deprivation, depression, anxiety, burnout, thyroid issues. The treatments for those are completely different, which is why the diagnosis matters before the pill.

One question changes the picture more than anything else: **were you like this as a kid?** ADHD is neurodevelopmental — it shows up by age 12, in school, everywhere, not just at work. If you were the scattered ten-year-old who forgot homework, ADHD evaluation is the right first door. If you were fine until a few years ago, look hard at sleep, mood, and stress first, because adult-onset focus collapse is rarely ADHD.

On pills, honestly:

- What your coworkers are "on" is almost certainly prescription stimulants (methylphenidate — Ritalin/Concerta — or amphetamines where legal). They do work same-day *if you actually have ADHD*. They're prescription for real reasons: heart rate and blood pressure effects, anxiety amplification, dependency, and they can make an undiagnosed mood or anxiety problem worse.
- Borrowing or buying them without a diagnosis means medicating a condition nobody has confirmed while the actual cause goes untreated. Bad trade.
- Supplements: mostly no. Omega-3 has modest evidence as an add-on for diagnosed ADHD, not as a standalone fix. Caffeine is the only over-the-counter thing that works fast — and it's a trap long-term, because it wrecks sleep, and bad sleep perfectly mimics ADHD. A lot of people are stuck in exactly that loop.
- Sleep is the boring one worth checking first: 7–9 hours, consistent times, for two weeks before concluding anything.

Concrete move: book a psychiatric assessment — self-referral works in most countries, usually 1–3 visits. If it is ADHD, you get the medication that actually works through the front door. If it isn't, you've just avoided stimulanting your way through a sleep or mood problem and masking it for years.

So, one thing you can do in the next ten minutes: ask yourself (or a parent) whether you were like this at age 10. That answer decides which door to knock on first.
