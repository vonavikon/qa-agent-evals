What you're looking for is git's reset command with the soft option, pointed at the commit just before your latest one, which git refers to as head tilde one. That winds your branch back one commit and puts every change from it straight into the staging area, as if you had just finished adding the files and were about to commit. Nothing is touched on disk, nothing is lost, and you can commit again right away or keep tinkering first.

There's a subtle variation depending on what you mean by keeping the changes. If you leave the option off and just reset back one commit, git does the same undo but also clears the staging area, so your edits end up in the working directory unstaged. That's the default behavior, and it matches your phrasing most literally: files exactly as they were, nothing committed, nothing staged. The soft version keeps everything staged instead, which saves you re-adding files if you plan to commit again immediately. Both are safe, so pick based on whether you want things staged or not.

The one thing to steer clear of is the hard option on that same command. It also moves the branch back a commit, but it deletes your changes in the process, which is precisely the outcome you're trying to avoid.

One last thing worth knowing: if that commit has already been pushed to a shared remote, undoing it locally rewrites history. Your next push will have to overwrite the remote's version, and if a colleague already pulled the commit, their history will disagree with yours, so a quick warning to them saves everyone a headache.
