Straight answer: no one can honestly give you a yes/no from that list, and you should distrust anyone who does. Focus problems, unfinished projects, lateness, and inbox chaos show up in ADHD, and also in sleep deprivation, depression, anxiety, burnout, and ordinary overload. Telling those apart is exactly what an evaluation does.

Two things separate real ADHD from the imitators:

- It starts in childhood (before 12) and shows up everywhere, not just at your current job. If you could focus fine in school and this started two years ago, that points away from ADHD and toward something else.
- It causes actual damage: lost jobs, failed courses, relationships that broke over it. Annoying is not the same as impairing.

The path to a real answer:

1. Take the ASRS screener. Six questions, two minutes, free. Search "ASRS v1.1". It is a screener, not a diagnosis, but a high score means an evaluation is worth booking.
2. If the score is high: book a psychiatrist, or ask your GP for a referral. Waiting lists run a few weeks; the evaluation itself is one or two sessions. Dig up old school reports if you can. Childhood evidence is a required part of the diagnosis, and it speeds things up.
3. If the score is low: fix sleep first. Chronic short sleep reproduces your exact list, all four items.

On the pill question, since you asked me to be straight:

- Stimulants (methylphenidate, amphetamine-based ones) are the real thing. For diagnosed ADHD they work within an hour of the first dose and they're among the most effective treatments in psychiatry. They're prescription-only for concrete reasons: heart and blood pressure risks, dependency, dose tuning. That's what the evaluation is for.
- Non-stimulants (atomoxetine and similar) also work but take four to six weeks.
- Supplements: nothing over the counter has a fast, strong, reliably demonstrated effect on focus. Omega-3 shows a small effect at best. Most commercial "focus" stacks are caffeine plus marketing.
- The people at work who are genuinely on something are on prescriptions matched to their diagnosis and heart. Borrowing or buying stimulants to treat an undiagnosed attention problem trades a fixable question for real cardiac and dependency risk.
